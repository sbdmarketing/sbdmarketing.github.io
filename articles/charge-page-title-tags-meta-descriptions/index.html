Writing Title Tags and Meta Descriptions for Defense Charge Pages

Someone who searches for a DUI lawyer and a county name late at night sees a stack of headlines with a line or two of gray text under each one. That is the entire pitch a charge page gets before the click. The headline usually comes from the title tag. The gray text often comes from the meta description, although Google can pick other text from the page.

On many defense sites both were set once by a plugin and never touched again. The title reads like a keyword list and the description is blank or copied from the homepage. Fixing this is cheap and can be done page by page, as long as you check what Google actually shows before and after.

What Google does with a title tag

Google's documentation on title links says the title element is the main source for the headline in a result, but Google also looks at the main visible heading, anchor text pointing to the page and other text on the page. It is more likely to replace a title that is missing, stuffed with repeated keywords, shared across many pages or out of step with what the page covers.

That suggests a simple working rule. Write a title that describes the page honestly and agrees with the H1, and Google will usually keep it. There is no published character limit. Titles get cut to fit the width of the screen, so the words that matter belong at the front. A firm name at the end may be trimmed on phones, and that is fine.

A pattern for charge page titles

A person scanning results wants to know whether the page is about their charge and whether the lawyer works near their court. A pattern that answers both looks like this:

  • The charge in the words searchers use, such as DUI or domestic violence
  • The city or county the page is built for
  • A short label such as defense lawyer or attorney
  • The firm name last, if space allows

A page might read "Felony DUI Defense Lawyer in Sacramento County | Firm Name." Compare that with "Sacramento DUI Attorney, DUI Lawyer, Drunk Driving Lawyer Sacramento." The second version repeats itself and tells the reader nothing new after the first few words. It also looks written for a machine, which is the kind of title Google tends to rewrite.

Use the charge name your searchers type, which may differ from the statute. One state calls it operating while intoxicated and another calls it driving under the influence. The Queries report for the page in Search Console shows which wording earns impressions. If the formal term does not fit in the title, put it in the H1 or the first paragraph.

Words like "best" or "top" deserve caution. Many state bars restrict comparative or unverifiable claims in lawyer advertising, and a title tag is advertising like any other text on the site. Check your state bar's advertising rules before using them.

Writing the meta description

Think of the meta description as a short reason to click. Google's snippet documentation says it may use the description when it describes the page better than other text, and it often pulls a passage from the page instead, especially when the search is more specific than the description. There is no fixed length either. Snippets are trimmed to fit the device.

Two sentences are usually enough for a charge page. The first says what the page covers in plain words. The second gives a concrete reason to keep reading, such as what happens at the first court date or what a lawyer looks at after an arrest. For example: "Charged with a second DUI in Placer County? Read what the court can impose and how a defense lawyer reviews the traffic stop and the breath test."

Avoid promises about outcomes here for the same reason you avoid them on the page.

Checking what searchers actually see

Do not assume your title is the one that shows. Search the page's main query on a phone in a private window and look at the result. A site: search on your domain with the charge name added shows how Google presents each related page side by side, which makes duplicates obvious.

If Google has replaced a title, look for the cause before rewriting. Common ones are a title that disagrees with the H1, a title shared with other pages, or a long tagline repeated on every page. After a change, compare click-through rate in the Performance report for the same queries over a few weeks before and after. Position changes also move click rates, so read the two numbers together and change one page at a time.

Sites with dozens of charge and county pages often build titles from a template. That works when the template pulls the charge and the location from separate fields. It fails when every county page for the same charge ends up with nearly the same title. A crawler export sorted by title shows those duplicates in a few minutes.

When to leave a title alone

A page that ranks well and earns a healthy click rate for the right queries does not need a new title because a tool flagged its length. Change a title when the page gets impressions but few clicks, when Google is showing a rewrite that misleads readers, or when the page's focus has changed.

If the whole page is due for a rewrite, handle the title as part of that work. A checklist for auditing a criminal charge page before a rewrite helps you record the current title, rankings and clicks first, so you can tell afterward whether the changes helped.

Questions about charge page titles

Should the firm name come first in a title tag?

Usually not on charge pages. The searcher is looking for their charge and their court, so those words belong at the front. The homepage and the about page are better places to lead with the firm name.

How often should a title tag be changed?

Only when there is a reason, such as low clicks on a page with plenty of impressions. Frequent changes make it hard to tell what worked.

Does the meta description need to repeat the keyword?

Including the charge name once, in a natural sentence, is enough. Repeating it reads badly to the person deciding whether to click.
