What Google's New Ranking Hub Means for Criminal Defense Sites

On November 24, 2026, Google added a small set of ranking pages to Search Central. One explains the principles Google says it uses to rank results. Another covers how site owners can stay informed about ranking updates, and a third explains how to send feedback about ranking changes. The same day, the November 2026 core update finished rolling out after roughly two weeks, and Google said a December core update is coming.

For a criminal defense firm, none of this changes what a good charge page looks like. It does change how a firm can follow updates, how it should react to a traffic drop, and what it can tell Google when something looks wrong. Here is what the announcements say in plain terms and what is worth doing before the December update starts.

What the ranking hub contains

The new pages sit in the ranking section of Google's Search Central documentation. The principles page says users come first and that every type of result, including AI Overviews, image results and video carousels, is judged by the same standard of usefulness. It also says Google does not adjust individual results by hand, apart from cases tied to legal obligations or its content policies.

That last point matters to lawyers who suspect a rival is being favored. Google's stated position is that rankings come from automated systems. If your firm slips, the useful question is what those systems now prefer, and whether your page still answers the search better than the pages above it.

The second page explains how Google announces ranking updates and how much notice it gives for changes it chooses to announce. The third page describes a feedback route through Search Console.

The December core update notice

Google rarely tells site owners about a core update before it begins. This time it did. According to coverage of the announcement, the December 2026 update is meant to improve how Google's systems understand and rank content for longer, more complex questions, along with personalized results. It applies to all regions and languages. Google has not given a start date and said it will confirm the rollout on the day it begins.

Longer, more complex questions describe a lot of criminal defense searching. A worried parent rarely types a two-word phrase. They write something closer to "my son was arrested for a DUI at 19 and it was his first offense, will he lose his license." Pages that answer that kind of layered question directly, in plain language, are the ones most likely to benefit if Google's systems get better at matching those queries.

Using the feedback route without false hope

Verified Search Console owners can send feedback about a ranking update, tagged with the update's name. Reports describe Google reviewing this feedback in aggregate to improve its systems. It will not move a single URL back up because a firm asked.

That makes the feedback form a place for clear, specific observations, such as a pattern of results that send people to the wrong state's law. It is a poor place to argue that your firm deserves a higher spot. If you send feedback, keep it short, describe what a searcher would see and why it fails them, and keep a copy so your team remembers what was reported.

A separate page on generative AI controls

Google also published a page that gathers the controls a site has over how its content is used by Google's generative AI features. Some of these controls are older. Google-Extended, for example, is a robots.txt token that lets a site limit use of its content in certain Google AI products, and Google has long said it does not affect inclusion or ranking in Search. Snippet controls and noindex are listed as well.

Before changing any of these, a defense firm should decide what it wants. Most firms want their charge pages quoted accurately in AI answers, with a link. Blocking content from AI features can reduce that exposure. For a longer look at the tradeoff, see this piece on why defense firms should think twice before opting out of Google's generative AI features.

What to check before the December rollout

You do not need to rebuild anything. A short review now gives you a clean baseline, so you can tell later whether the update helped or hurt.

  • Export the last three months of Search Console data for your charge pages, by page and by query, and save it with today's date.
  • Read your top five charge pages as a stranger would and confirm each one answers the main question in the opening paragraphs.
  • Look for long questions in your query data and note which pages they land on.
  • Fix outdated penalty ranges, court addresses or fee language before the update, not after.
  • Write down any site changes you make, with dates, so they are not confused with the update's effects.

If traffic shifts once the rollout starts, wait until Google says it has finished before drawing conclusions. Core updates often move rankings up and down for days. Comparing your saved baseline with the period after completion tells you far more than daily checks.

Questions about the ranking hub and the December update

Will Google's ranking feedback form help my firm recover lost rankings?

Probably not directly. Reports describe the feedback as reviewed in aggregate to improve Google's systems, with no changes made to individual pages on request.

Does the December core update target law firms?

Nothing in Google's notice singles out legal sites. The stated focus is longer, more complex questions and personalized results across all topics.

Should we block Google-Extended to protect our content?

Only after weighing what you would give up. Google says Google-Extended does not affect Search inclusion or ranking, but it can limit how your content is used in some Google AI products where clients may be asking questions.
