Reading Year-Over-Year Search Data for Seasonal Criminal Charges

A DUI page that drew fewer clicks in February than in December has not necessarily lost ground. Searches for many criminal charges rise and fall with the calendar, and a defense lawyer who compares one month with the month before will often read normal seasonal movement as a problem. The opposite happens too: a real decline can hide inside a busy season.

The fix is a habit more than a tool. Compare each charge page with the same weeks a year earlier, look at the right measures, and keep a short record of what happened in your market. This article walks through how to do that in Google Search Console with data most firms already have.

Why month-to-month comparisons mislead defense firms

Criminal defense search demand follows events that repeat. Holiday weekends, college move-in week, summer boating season, big local games and the weeks around New Year's Eve can all change how many people in your county search for a given charge. If your report shows clicks to a domestic violence page falling from one month to the next, that number alone tells you very little.

A year-over-year view asks a better question: did this page do better or worse than it did during the same weeks last year? That comparison holds the season roughly steady, so whatever difference remains is more likely to come from your rankings and your competitors.

It still has limits. One year of history is thin, and a single odd event, such as a highway closure that moved traffic stops to another county, can bend a month. Treat the comparison as a source of questions rather than a verdict.

Setting up the comparison in Search Console

The Performance report in Search Console lets you compare two date ranges. Pick a range such as the last three months, then choose the option to compare it with the same period a year earlier. Turn on clicks, impressions, average position and click-through rate so all four appear on the chart.

Then filter by page. Look at one charge page at a time rather than the whole site, because a strong month for your DUI page can hide a weak month for your theft page. If your firm has pages for individual counties or courthouses, check those separately.

Weeks are often more useful than months for seasonal charges. A holiday that fell on a Thursday one year and a Saturday the next can shift traffic between weeks, so when something looks odd, narrow the range to the specific days around the event.

Reading impressions, clicks and position together

Each measure answers a different question. Read side by side, they keep you away from the most common mistakes.

  • Impressions rising while position stays flat usually means more people searched. That is demand, and your page did not earn it.
  • Position improving while impressions hold steady usually means your page is beating competitors for the same demand.
  • Clicks falling while impressions and position hold steady points to the results page itself. More ads, a changed map pack or an AI summary above the results can take clicks without your ranking moving.
  • Impressions and position falling together is the pattern that most often needs work on the page.

Average position across many queries hides a lot. Open the Queries tab for the page and check the handful of searches that bring most of its clicks. Those are the terms that produce calls.

Keeping a calendar of local events

Search data makes far more sense next to a plain list of what happened. Keep a shared document with a line for each month. Note local festivals, checkpoints announced in the news, laws that took effect, a competitor's new office and every change you made to your own site. It takes about ten minutes a month.

A year from now, when the October numbers look strange, a note saying the contact page was redesigned that month, or that a campus event moved to a different weekend, will save hours of guessing. Without it, people tend to credit or blame whatever they changed most recently.

Searches that include your firm's name behave differently from charge searches, so keep them apart. A guide to separating branded from unbranded searches in Search Console shows how to split the two so referral and reputation traffic does not blur the seasonal picture for your charge pages.

Turning the comparison into a monthly decision

The point of the exercise is deciding what to work on next. A simple rule helps. If a page lost position year over year and the drop lasted more than a few weeks, review that page first. If a page held position but lost clicks, search its main terms in a private browser window to see what changed above you. If impressions and clicks both grew, leave the page alone and spend the time elsewhere.

Write each decision down next to the event calendar. Over a year you build a record of what you tried and what followed, and that record is worth more than any single report. Questions about seasonal search data

How much history do I need before year-over-year comparisons help?

At least 13 months, so the current month has a match from last year. Search Console keeps about 16 months of performance data, so export it now and then if you want a longer record.

Should I rewrite a charge page during its busy season?

Small fixes are fine. Large rewrites are easier to judge when made before or after the busy weeks, because heavy seasonal demand makes it hard to tell what caused a change.

What if my site is too new for a full year of data?

Use Google Trends to see the seasonal shape of searches for your main charges in your state. Compare your own numbers against that shape until you have a year of history.
