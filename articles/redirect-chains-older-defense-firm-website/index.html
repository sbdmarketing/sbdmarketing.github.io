Cleaning Up Redirect Chains on an Older Defense Firm Website

Most defense firm websites that have been around for ten years carry some history. The firm changed its name when a partner left. The site moved from http to https, then from a www address to a bare domain. A developer rebuilt the practice area section and renamed every DUI page. Each change added a redirect, and nobody went back to clean up the older ones.

The result is a redirect chain: an old URL that sends visitors to a second URL, which sends them to a third, which finally lands on the live charge page. A person clicking through barely notices. Search crawlers notice more, and links that other sites pointed at your old pages have to pass through another hop each time the site changes.

How a chain forms on a law firm site

Picture a page about felony drug possession. In 2016 it lived at /practice-areas/drug-crimes.html. A 2019 redesign moved it to /drug-charges/, with a redirect from the old address. In 2022 the firm switched to https and dropped the www. In 2025 the page was split into possession and trafficking pages, and /drug-charges/ was redirected to the possession page.

Now a link from a 2017 bar association article hits the http www version of the 2016 URL. That request may pass through four or five redirects before it reaches a page. Each rebuild was handled correctly on its own terms. The trouble is that each one only pointed the previous address forward by one step.

What Google says about redirect hops

Google's documentation on HTTP status codes says its crawlers follow up to 10 redirect hops, and past that the URL is reported as a redirect error in Search Console. Most firm sites never get near ten. That limit is a ceiling, though, and Google staff have said publicly for years that fewer hops are better, with a common suggestion to keep chains under five.

Shorter chains help in less obvious ways too. Each hop adds a round trip on a phone with a weak signal, which is how many people read a defense site in a hallway at the jail or in a parked car. Each hop is also another place for a mistake to creep in, such as a temporary 302 where a permanent 301 was meant, or a loop that sends the crawler back to where it started.

Finding the chains

You need a list of every old URL that might still be requested. Good sources:

  • The redirect rules on your server or in your CMS plugin
  • Old XML sitemaps saved from past versions of the site
  • The pages report in Search Console, filtered for redirects
  • Backlink reports showing which old URLs other sites link to
  • Your analytics landing page report from before each redesign

Run that list through a crawler or a simple script that follows redirects one step at a time and records each hop. You want three columns at the end: the starting URL, the number of hops, and the final destination with its status code.

Sort by hop count. Anything with two or more hops is a chain worth fixing. Anything that ends in a 404, or in a redirect to the homepage for a page that once covered a specific charge, deserves its own look.

Fixing them without breaking anything

The fix for a chain is to point every old URL straight at the final destination in a single 301. In the drug possession example, the 2016 address, the 2019 address and every http and www variant should all redirect directly to the current possession page.

A few rules keep this safe:

  1. Export your current redirect rules before you change them, so you can roll back.
  2. Update rules from the oldest outward, and handle the protocol and host change (http to https, www to bare) in one combined step where your server allows it.
  3. Send each old charge page to the closest current page about the same charge, never to the homepage by default.
  4. Re-run the crawl after deploying and confirm every starting URL now reaches a 200 page in one hop.
  5. Update internal links in your own content so they point at final URLs and never rely on a redirect at all.

That last step matters more than it sounds. Old blog posts on defense sites often link to charge pages by their 2016 addresses. Fixing those links removes the chain from your own site completely, and it costs nothing. Keeping chains from coming back

The cheapest chain to fix is the one that never forms. Before any rebuild, keep a spreadsheet of every live URL and its planned new address. When you add redirects for the rebuild, search the existing rules for any that point at a URL you are about to retire, and update them in the same release.

Assign one person to own redirect rules, even at a three-lawyer firm. Agencies and freelancers change over the years, and chains often start when a new developer adds rules on top of the old ones without reading them. A short note in your site documentation saying where redirects live and who changes them saves the next person an afternoon.

Questions defense lawyers ask about redirect chains

Will fixing redirect chains raise my rankings?

It can help pages that have old links pointing at them, and it removes a source of crawl errors. It rarely moves rankings by itself. Think of it as maintenance that lets your other SEO work count fully.

Should I ever remove old redirects?

Keep redirects for any URL that still has outside links or still gets visits. Rules for URLs with no links and no traffic for several years can be retired, but there is little cost in keeping them if they point straight to the right page.

How often should we check for chains?

After every redesign or URL change, and once or twice a year otherwise. With a crawler it takes an hour or two.
