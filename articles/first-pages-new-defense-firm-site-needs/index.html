The First Pages a New Defense Firm Site Needs to Show Up in Google

A lawyer who opens a criminal defense practice usually gets a website before getting a marketing plan. A designer builds a homepage, an about page and a contact form, and then the site sits there. Months later the firm still gets almost no calls from Google, and nobody can say why.

The reason is usually simple. Google ranks pages, and a person searching for help with a specific charge in a specific city needs a page that answers that exact question. If the page does not exist, there is nothing to rank. Below are the pages a new defense firm site needs first, roughly in the order we would build them, and what each one has to contain.

The homepage: say who you help and where

The homepage still matters. It is the page other websites link to most often, and it tells both Google and visitors what kind of firm this is. Keep it plain:

  • Name the type of law you practice in the first sentence.
  • Name the city and the counties or courts you cover.
  • Link to each charge page with clear anchor text such as "DUI defense in Placer County."
  • Show a phone number that can be tapped on a phone.

Slogans make weak headlines. "Aggressive. Experienced. Trusted." tells a search engine nothing about where you practice or what you handle, and it tells a frightened reader very little either.

One page for each charge you want to be hired for

Charge pages are where most new cases come from in search. Pick the five to eight charges you most want to handle and that people near you actually search for. For many solo practices the list includes DUI, domestic violence, drug possession, theft and probation violations, but yours should reflect your own practice and your local courts.

A useful charge page usually covers these points:

  1. What the charge means under your state's law, in plain language, with the statute number.
  2. The range of possible penalties, stated carefully and accurately.
  3. What happens next in your local courts, step by step.
  4. Issues and defenses you commonly look at, without promising outcomes.
  5. How to reach you and what to bring to a first meeting.

Write these yourself, or review every line if someone else drafts them. A page with a wrong penalty or an outdated statute misleads the person reading it, and readers who know the law will notice.

An attorney bio that shows a real person wrote the pages

Google's guidance on creating helpful content asks site owners to make it clear who created a page and what experience stands behind it. On a law firm site, the attorney bio is where that evidence lives. List your law school, the year you were admitted, the courts you appear in, any time spent as a prosecutor or public defender, and your bar memberships. Then link the bio from each charge page so a reader can see who is behind the advice.

A bio also answers a question every caller has and few ask out loud: is this lawyer someone who goes to my courthouse every week?

A contact page and a Google Business Profile that match

Local search leans heavily on consistent basic facts. Your contact page should show the firm name, office address, phone number and hours exactly as they appear on your Google Business Profile. If you share a suite or rent a virtual office, read Google's Business Profile guidelines before you create a listing, because listings at addresses where nobody from the firm works can be suspended.

The profile lives outside your website, but it belongs on this list anyway. For many local searches the map results appear above the regular results, and your profile sends people back to your homepage or a location page.

Pages that can wait

New owners often spend early effort on work that pays off later, if at all:

  • A blog with weekly news posts. Helpful down the road, but charge pages come first.
  • Pages for charges you rarely take. They pull attention away from your main work.
  • Dozens of city pages where only the place name changes. Near-duplicate pages seldom rank and can drag down how Google sees the rest of the site.

Once the core pages are live and indexed, add depth. Good next steps are a page for each courthouse you appear in, a process page about what happens after an arrest, and answers to questions you hear on real calls.

Make sure Google can find what you built

Set up Google Search Console the day the site launches. Submit your sitemap, then watch the Pages report over the next few weeks to confirm your charge pages are indexed. If a page you care about is missing, the URL Inspection tool will usually tell you why.

Questions new defense lawyers ask about these first pages

How long before these pages bring in calls?

It depends on competition in your city and the age of your domain. In a smaller market, a well-written charge page can start appearing for some searches within a few months. In a large metro area it usually takes longer. Search Console impressions tend to move before the phone does.

Should I write the pages myself?

You know the law and the local courts better than any hired writer. Many lawyers outline the facts and local details, have a writer turn that into readable copy, and then review the final draft line by line. Before anything goes live, check your state bar's advertising rules, since some states restrict words like "specialist" and require certain disclaimers.

Do I need a separate page for each county?

Only if you have something different to say about each one, such as the courthouse address, how arraignments are scheduled or which diversion programs are offered there. If the county name would be the only difference, a single page covering your service area is the better choice.
