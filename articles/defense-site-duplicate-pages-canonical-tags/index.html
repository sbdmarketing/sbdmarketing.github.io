Fixing Duplicate Pages and Canonical Tags on a Defense Firm Site

A defense firm site with forty pages can easily have two hundred URLs. Tracking parameters from ads, printer versions, tag archives from an old blog theme, the same page with and without a trailing slash, and the http version that never got redirected all count as separate addresses to a crawler. Most of them show content that already exists somewhere else on the site.

Google handles a lot of this on its own by grouping similar URLs and picking one to show. The trouble starts when it picks the wrong one, or when your own site sends mixed messages about which version you prefer. Then an outdated copy of your DUI page can be the one people see in search. This piece walks through how to find duplicates on a defense site and how to clean them up without breaking anything.

How Google decides which version counts

When Google finds several pages with the same or nearly the same content, it treats them as a group and chooses one as the canonical. That is the version it indexes and shows in results. The others are kept as alternates and rarely appear.

You can suggest a canonical with a rel="canonical" tag in the page head, with redirects, with the URLs you list in your sitemap and with the way you link internally. Google's documentation describes these as signals. A canonical tag is a strong hint, but Google can and does ignore it when other signals point somewhere else. If your sitemap lists one URL, your menu links to a second and the canonical tag names a third, you have given Google three answers and it will choose for itself.

Finding duplicates with tools you already have

Search Console is the place to start. In the Pages report, look for the reasons "Duplicate without user-selected canonical" and "Duplicate, Google chose different canonical than user." The second one deserves the most attention because it means Google looked at your instruction and disagreed.

For any important charge page, run the URL Inspection tool. It shows the canonical you declared next to the one Google selected. When those two match, you can move on. When they differ, Google has told you which page it thinks is the real one, and you can usually see why once you open both.

A desktop crawler fills in the rest. Crawl the site and sort by page title and by content similarity. On defense sites the same patterns come up again and again:

  • City pages built from one template with only the city name swapped
  • Old practice area pages left live after a redesign, sitting next to their replacements
  • URLs with ?utm_source or ?gclid parameters that got linked from somewhere public
  • Attorney bio pages that exist under both /team/ and /attorneys/

Fixing the common defense site patterns

Each pattern has a different fix, and choosing the right one matters more than acting fast.

If two pages cover the same charge and one is clearly older, redirect the old URL to the new one with a 301. This passes the old page's links along and removes the duplicate for good. A canonical tag would be the wrong tool here because both pages would stay live and visitors could still land on the outdated one.

For parameter URLs, a self-referencing canonical on every page handles most of the problem. Each page should name its own clean URL as canonical, so a visitor who arrives with a tracking parameter still points Google back to the plain version. Check that your CMS writes the canonical as an absolute URL with the correct protocol and host.

Trailing slash and http duplicates belong at the server level. Pick one form, redirect the others to it and make sure internal links use the chosen form. This is usually a single rule in the host's settings.

For bio pages that live under two paths, choose one, redirect the other and update the menu. If you are planning a bigger rebuild, this redirect plan for rebuilding a criminal defense law firm site covers how to map old URLs before anything goes live.

Near-copy city pages need a content decision

City pages are the hardest case because no tag fixes them. If your Oakland, Hayward and Fremont DUI pages share ninety percent of their text, Google may fold them together and show only one. Pointing a canonical from each city page to a single main page would tell Google to ignore the rest, which defeats the point of having them.

The real fix is to make each page earn its place. Name the courthouse that hears cases from that city, describe how arraignments are scheduled there, mention the local police agencies and note anything about the process that differs from the county next door. If you cannot write that much that is specific and true, the page probably should not exist, and you are better off merging it into a county page with a redirect.

Checking that the fix held

Changes to canonicals and redirects take time to show up. After you ship a fix, request indexing for the main pages through URL Inspection and watch the Pages report over the next few weeks. The count of duplicate URLs should fall, and the pages you care about should show your declared canonical as the Google-selected one.

Keep a dated log of each change so you can tell your fix apart from a Google update in the same month.

Questions about duplicate pages on defense sites

Will duplicate content get my firm penalized?

In most cases, no. Google's guidance says duplicate content within a site is common and usually just leads it to pick one version. The cost is that the version it picks may be the wrong one, and link value gets split across copies.

Should every page have a canonical tag?

A self-referencing canonical on every indexable page is a sensible default. It costs nothing and protects pages that pick up tracking parameters. Make sure the tag names the exact URL you want indexed.

Can I use noindex instead of a canonical?

You can, but they do different jobs. Noindex removes a page from results entirely, while a canonical consolidates signals into another page. For true duplicates, a redirect or canonical is usually the better choice.
