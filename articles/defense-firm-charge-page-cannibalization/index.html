Ending Keyword Cannibalization Between Defense Firm Charge Pages

Most defense firm websites grow by addition. A DUI page goes up at launch, then a blog post about first offense DUI, then a page about DUI with a minor in the car, then a city page that repeats half of the original DUI copy. Each piece made sense when it was written. A few years later Google is looking at five URLs that all seem to answer "DUI lawyer in Fresno," and it has to guess which one the firm means.

SEO people call this keyword cannibalization. The name sounds dramatic for what is usually a filing problem. The fix is mostly editorial: decide which page owns each search, then make every other page either support that owner or step aside.

How to tell if two pages are competing

Start in Google Search Console. Open the Performance report, filter by a query you care about, such as "assault lawyer" plus your city, and then click the Pages tab. If one URL collects nearly all the impressions for that query, you are fine. If two or three URLs split them, and the position for the query swings from week to week, Google has not settled on one answer.

A second sign shows up when you search the phrase yourself. If the page that ranks is a three-year-old blog post instead of the practice page you built for that exact charge, Google may be choosing the wrong page because the right one gives it weaker signals.

Do not treat every overlap as a problem. A page about felony DUI and a page about first offense DUI can both show for "DUI lawyer" without hurting each other, because each answers a narrower question well. The trouble starts when two pages try to answer the same question at the same depth.

Build a one-page-per-intent map

Open a spreadsheet and list every indexable URL on the site. Next to each one, write the single search it is meant to win, in the words a client would type. Keep it short: "domestic violence lawyer San Jose," "what happens at a DUI arraignment," "expungement eligibility California."

Then sort by that column. Duplicates jump out. You will often find a practice page, a city page and a blog post all assigned, in effect, to the same search. The map also shows gaps, like a charge the firm handles every week that has no page at all.

If your firm has never decided which charges deserve their own page, the earlier note on splitting first, second and felony DUI into separate pages walks through that decision for one charge family, and the same reasoning carries over to theft, assault and drug cases.

Decide which page wins

For each cluster of competing URLs, pick one owner. The best candidate is usually the page with the most useful content and the most links pointing at it, both from inside the site and from other sites. Search Console's Links report and your own crawl will show you which page has the stronger link profile.

Sometimes the winner is obvious and sometimes it is the blog post, which can be awkward. If an old article has earned links from local news coverage, it may be wiser to fold the practice page's content into that URL than to throw those links away. Go with the evidence rather than the site's original plan.

Merge, redirect or refocus the others

Each losing page gets one of three treatments.

  1. Merge and redirect. If the page adds little that the owner lacks, move any useful paragraphs into the owner, then send a 301 redirect from the old URL to the owner. Update internal links so they point straight to the owner instead of through the redirect.
  2. Refocus. If the page could serve a narrower question, rewrite it for that question. A second DUI blog post might become a page about license suspension hearings, which is a real search with its own audience.
  3. Keep and canonicalize. For near-duplicates that must stay live, such as a printable version, a rel="canonical" tag pointing at the owner can help. Google treats canonical tags as a strong hint, not an order, so this works best when the pages really are close copies.

Avoid deleting pages and leaving 404s where a redirect would make sense. Clients still have old links saved, and other sites may link to those URLs.

Use internal links to confirm the choice

Once the owner is chosen, the rest of the site should agree with it. Every mention of "DUI defense" in body copy should link to the DUI owner page with plain anchor text that names the charge. City pages should link to the charge owner instead of repeating its explanation.

Navigation matters too. If the main menu points at a different URL than the one you picked, Google receives mixed signals from the most prominent links on every page. Fix the menu first.

Check the result after a few weeks

Rankings take time to settle after redirects and rewrites. Go back to the same Search Console query in three to six weeks and compare. You want to see one URL holding most impressions and a steadier average position. If two pages are still splitting the query, look for leftover internal links or a sitemap that still lists the redirected URL.

Write down what you changed and when. A simple change log makes it far easier to explain a ranking movement later, and it keeps the next person who edits the site from recreating the same overlap.

Questions about competing charge pages

Will merging pages lower my traffic?

Usually traffic to the combined page rises over time, because signals that were split now point at one URL. Expect some movement in the first weeks while Google processes the redirects.

Should every city get its own page for each charge?

Only when you have something specific to say about that city, such as the courthouse, local procedures or nearby jails. Pages that only swap the city name tend to compete with each other and rarely rank well.

Can a blog post and a practice page target the same charge?

Yes, if they answer different questions. Let the practice page target the hiring search and have the post answer a specific question, such as what to expect at a first court date, linking back to the practice page.
