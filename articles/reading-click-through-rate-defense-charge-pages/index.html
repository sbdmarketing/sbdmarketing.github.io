Reading Click-Through Rate on Defense Charge Pages in Search Console

A charge page can sit in the top five results for months and still bring in fewer calls than a page ranked lower. The usual culprit shows up in one column of Search Console that many defense lawyers skip: click-through rate. It tells you how often people who saw your page in the results chose to open it.

Position gets the attention because it feels like a score. Click-through rate is closer to the moment a frightened person makes a choice. Reading it well takes a little care, because the number moves for reasons that have nothing to do with your page.

What the number actually measures

Search Console divides clicks by impressions. An impression is counted when your page appears in a result the searcher could see, even if they never scrolled to it. A click is counted when they open it from Google. So a 3 percent rate on a page with 2,000 impressions means about 60 people came through.

The average position beside it is also an average. A page that shows at position 2 for one phrase and position 40 for another can report an average near 20, which describes neither situation. That is why a page-level rate is a blunt tool. The useful view is the page filtered down to its main queries.

Setting up a fair comparison

Open the Performance report, set a date range of at least three months, and switch on all four metrics. Then click into a single charge page and look at the Queries tab. Sort by impressions so the phrases that matter most sit at the top.

Before you compare anything, split branded searches out. People who type your firm's name or a lawyer's name click at very high rates, and they can make a weak page look healthy. If your property has the branded filter, use it. If not, add a query filter that excludes your name and its common misspellings. Our guide to pulling Search Console data by charge page with the API shows how to do the same split in a script if you track many pages.

Now you have something worth reading: the unbranded queries for one page, each with its own position and rate.

Why a good position can still earn few clicks

When a page ranks well and rarely gets opened, look at the results page itself before you rewrite anything. Search the phrase in a private window from your city on a phone. Several things above your result can absorb the click:

  • Paid ads, which on DUI and drug charge searches in larger cities can fill the top of the screen.
  • The map pack, which often answers a "near me" or city search before any website result appears.
  • An AI Overview that answers the basic question, such as whether a first offense is a misdemeanor in your state.
  • Directory listings that look more neutral to someone comparing lawyers.

None of that is your page's fault, and some of it you can only answer by working on your Google Business Profile or your reviews. It does tell you not to judge a page only against the rate you would expect for its position.

Fixes that tend to help

When the results page is fair and the rate is still low, the snippet is usually the problem. Google often writes the snippet from your title tag and meta description, though it can pull other text from the page when it thinks that text matches the query better.

Look at your title as a searcher would. "DUI Lawyer | Smith Law" says very little. A title that names the charge, the county and something specific about how you handle it gives a worried reader a reason to pick it. The meta description can say what the page covers in plain terms: penalties, the license hearing deadline, what happens at the first court date.

Then check whether Google is showing your words at all. If the snippet in the live result is a random sentence from the middle of the page, your description may not match what people are searching. Rewrite it around the query that brings the most impressions.

Change one page at a time and write down the date. Search Console data arrives with a delay of a couple of days, and a small page needs several weeks before a change in rate means anything.

When the rate is high but calls are low

The opposite pattern matters too. A page with a strong rate and few calls is winning the click and losing the reader. That points to the page itself: a slow load on mobile, a phone number buried below a long introduction, or copy that does not answer the question the snippet promised.

Compare the query list against the page content. If people arrive searching "second DUI jail time" and the page opens with your firm history, they leave. Match the first screen to the reason they clicked.

A simple monthly routine

Pick your five most important charge pages. Once a month, record for each one the unbranded impressions, clicks, rate and average position for its top three queries. Note anything you changed and anything you saw on the live results page, such as a new ad block or an AI Overview appearing.

After three or four months the pattern becomes clear. Some pages need better snippets, some need work on the page, and some sit behind features you cannot outrank. Knowing which is which saves you from rewriting pages that were never the problem.

Questions about click-through rate on charge pages

What is a good click-through rate for a defense charge page?

There is no fixed benchmark, because the rate depends on position, ads, the map pack and AI features on each results page. Compare a page against its own history and against your other pages for similar searches.

Should I rewrite the title tag on every page with a low rate?

No. Check the live results first. If ads, maps or an AI Overview sit above you, a new title may not change much. Rewrite titles where the results page gives you a fair chance and the snippet looks weak.

How long should I wait after changing a title or description?

Give it several weeks, longer for low-traffic pages. Google has to recrawl the page, and small daily numbers swing a lot on their own.
