Internal Links That Help Charge Pages Rank for a Defense Firm

Most defense firm websites have the pages they need. There is a DUI page, a domestic violence page, a drug possession page and maybe a dozen more. What many of them lack is a sensible set of links between those pages. The homepage points to a practice areas list, the list points to each charge, and then every charge page sits alone with nothing leading in or out except the main menu.

Internal links are the links from one page of your site to another. Google uses them to find pages, to judge which pages you treat as important and to understand what each page is about from the words in the link. A firm can change all of this without asking anyone else for anything, which makes it one of the cheaper SEO projects a defense practice can take on. Here is how to plan it.

Start with a map of what you already have

Before adding a single link, list every page on the site in a spreadsheet. Search Console's Pages report and a crawl from a desktop tool will give you the URLs. For each one, note the charge or topic it covers and how many other pages on your site link to it.

The pattern that shows up on defense sites is predictable. The homepage and the contact page get links from every page through the header and footer. Older blog posts collect a few links. The charge pages that bring in your best cases often get one link from a menu and nothing from the body text of any other page.

Mark the pages you most want to rank. If you have not decided which charges matter most to the business, settle that first. This piece on deciding which charges your firm should market first walks through that choice, and the answer shapes everything below.

Group pages the way clients think about their case

A person charged with a second DUI does not think in terms of your site structure. They think about their license, the jail time they might face and whether the first conviction counts against them. Your links should follow those questions.

Build small clusters. A DUI cluster might hold the main DUI page, a page on second offenses, a page on refusing a breath test, a page on license hearings and any blog posts that answer DUI questions. Each page in the cluster should link to the main DUI page, and the main page should link out to each of the others where the text naturally mentions them.

Clusters do not need to be large. Three to six pages that link to each other with clear wording are enough to tell Google that the firm covers the subject in depth.

Write anchor text that names the charge

The clickable words of a link are its anchor text. Google reads them as a hint about the page on the other end. "Click here" and "learn more" tell it nothing. A phrase such as "penalties for a felony DUI" tells both Google and the reader where the link goes.

Vary the wording. If every link to your DUI page reads "Houston DUI lawyer," the pattern looks forced and reads badly. Use the language that fits the sentence, such as "how a DUI arrest affects your license" in one place and "our DUI defense page" in another.

Put links where people read

Links in the body of a page carry more weight with readers than links buried in a footer. They also give Google more context because of the sentences around them.

Good places for internal links on a defense site include:

  • the paragraph on a charge page that describes the penalties, linking to a page on related or aggravated charges
  • the section on what happens after an arrest, linking to your bail or arraignment page
  • FAQ answers that mention another charge or process
  • blog posts, which should almost always link to the charge page they relate to
  • attorney bios, linking to the charges that lawyer handles most

A useful rule of thumb is that every page should link to at least two or three others in its body text, and every important page should receive links from several others. A charge page with forty links scattered through its text becomes hard to read, so stop when the links stop helping a reader.

Fix the links that leak

Internal linking also means cleaning up what is broken. Run a crawl and look for:

  1. Links that point to pages returning a 404 error.
  2. Links that pass through one or more redirects before reaching the final page.
  3. Pages with no internal links at all, often called orphan pages.
  4. Links that point to the http version of a page or to an old URL structure.

Update the links themselves rather than relying on redirects. Orphan pages either need links from relevant pages or, if they are thin and out of date, they may be candidates for merging into a stronger page.

Keep it current as the site grows

Every new page should be linked from at least one existing page on the day it goes live, and it should link back to the main page of its cluster. Make this part of the publishing checklist so it does not depend on memory.

Review the map every few months. When a new blog post covers a question about probation violations, add a link to it from your probation page. When a law changes and a page gets rewritten, check that the links in it still make sense.

Questions defense lawyers ask about internal links

Do links in the main menu count as internal links?

Yes. Menu and footer links appear on every page, so they help Google find pages, but they say little about how pages relate. Links in the body text do more to show which pages belong together.

Should every blog post link to a charge page?

Almost always. If a post answers a question about a charge you defend, a link to that charge page helps the reader take the next step and shows Google which page is the main source on the subject.

Can too many internal links hurt a page?

They can make a page harder to read, and that matters more than any penalty. If a reader would not click the link, or the link interrupts every sentence, remove it.
