A Two-Hour Weekly SEO Routine for a Defense Lawyer New to Marketing

Most defense lawyers who start paying attention to SEO do it in bursts. A slow month arrives, someone reads three articles about Google rankings, and a weekend disappears into rewriting the homepage. Then trial prep takes over and nothing gets touched for four months.

A short routine done every week works better than those bursts. Search engines reward sites that stay accurate and keep growing in useful ways, and a lawyer who spends two focused hours a week on the basics will usually see more progress over a year than one who spends a frantic weekend each quarter. What follows is a routine built for someone with no marketing background and a full caseload.

Block the time like a court date

Put two hours on the calendar at the same time every week. Friday afternoon works for a lot of solo practitioners because the court week is mostly done. Treat it the way you would treat a hearing: it gets moved only for something that cannot wait.

Split the time into four parts. Spend about 20 minutes on data, 20 on your Google Business Profile, an hour on one page of your site, and the last 20 minutes on notes for next week. The split matters less than the habit. If a week only allows one hour, do the page work and skip the rest.

Twenty minutes in Search Console

Google Search Console is free and shows which searches your pages appear for, how often people click and whether Google has trouble reading your site. If you have never set it up, that is the first week's whole job. A short guide to your first week in Google Search Console walks through verification and the reports worth opening.

Once it is running, check three things each week:

  • The Performance report filtered to the last 28 days, sorted by impressions, to see which charges people find you for.
  • The Pages report, to spot pages Google has not indexed.
  • Any new messages in the inbox, which is where Google reports security and manual action problems.

Write down one observation in a plain document. Something like "DUI page showing for 'second DUI' searches but few clicks" is enough. Over a few months those notes become the map of where to work.

Twenty minutes on your Google Business Profile

For a local defense practice, the map results often matter as much as the regular listings. Each week, open the profile and check that hours, phone number and website link are still right. Answer any new reviews in a calm, short way that confirms nothing about the reviewer's case. Many state bars have warned lawyers against revealing client information in review replies, so read your own state's guidance before you write.

Add a photo or a post every couple of weeks if you have something real to share: a new associate, a change in office hours, a talk you gave at a community event. Avoid stock images that could belong to any firm.

One hour on one page

This is where rankings actually move. Pick a single page per week and improve it. Start with the charges you most want to defend, then work outward.

A useful order for a new marketer:

  1. Read the page on your phone as if you had just been arrested. Note anything confusing.
  2. Check that the page names the charge, the city or county and the court in plain words near the top.
  3. Answer the questions clients ask on the first call, in the words they use.
  4. Correct anything outdated, such as statute numbers, fines or procedures that have changed in your state.
  5. Add a link to one or two related pages on your own site.

An hour will not finish a long page. That is fine. Finish the most important part and note where to pick up next week.

Twenty minutes of notes and planning

End each session by writing three lines: what you changed, what you noticed and what you will do next week. Date the entry. When rankings shift a month later, you will be able to connect the change to something specific instead of guessing.

This is also the time to keep a short list of page ideas. Every time a client asks a question you have not answered on the site, add it. Those questions come from real people with real cases, which makes them better topics than anything a keyword tool suggests on its own.

What to leave for later

New marketers often get pulled toward tasks that feel technical and important but pay off slowly for a small firm. Chasing a perfect page speed score, buying directory listings in bulk and rewriting every page at once can all wait. So can link building schemes that promise fast results, which can cause real harm to a site and may also run into your state's advertising rules.

If you hire help later, your notes will make that relationship better. A good agency can pick up a site faster when the owner knows which pages matter, which searches bring calls and what has already been tried.

Questions lawyers ask about a weekly SEO routine

How long before the routine shows results?

It depends on your market, your competition and the condition of your site. Small fixes to indexing problems can show up within weeks. Rankings for competitive charges in a large city usually take months of steady work.

Should a paralegal or assistant do this instead?

They can handle the data checks and profile updates once you show them what to look for. The page work benefits from a lawyer's eye, because accuracy about charges and procedure matters more than anything else on a defense site.

Do I need paid SEO tools to start?

No. Search Console, your Google Business Profile dashboard and a plain notes document cover the first several months. Paid tools become useful once you are comparing yourself with competitors across many searches.
