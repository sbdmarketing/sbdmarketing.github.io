A Plain Explanation of How Google Ranks a Defense Firm's Pages

Most defense lawyers who start paying attention to marketing hear the same vocabulary in their first week. Vendors talk about crawling, indexing and rankings as if everyone learned the terms in law school. Nobody did, and the gap makes it hard to judge whether an SEO proposal makes sense or whether a monthly report shows any progress.

The basic process is easier to follow once you see it as a series of separate stages, each with its own ways to fail. Google's own documentation describes them as crawling, indexing and serving results. This article walks through each stage using the pages a criminal defense firm actually has, so you can tell which stage a problem belongs to before anyone spends money fixing it.

Stage one: Google has to find the page

Google discovers pages mostly by following links, from your own site and from other sites, and by reading sitemaps. A program called Googlebot requests the page and downloads it. If nothing links to a new DUI page and it is missing from your sitemap, Google may take a long time to find it, or may never find it at all.

For a defense firm, the most common discovery problem is the orphaned page. Someone adds a page for a new charge, publishes it and forgets to link it from the practice area menu or from related charge pages. The page exists, but nothing points to it.

A few things can block this stage, on purpose or by accident:

  • A robots.txt rule that tells crawlers to stay out of a folder
  • A password on a staging copy that was never removed when the site launched
  • Pages that load their text only after scripts run, which some crawlers handle poorly

Stage two: Google decides whether to keep it

After a page is crawled, Google analyzes it and decides whether to store it in its index. Being crawled does not guarantee being indexed. Google may skip pages it considers duplicates of another page, pages marked noindex and pages with very little useful content.

This is where many small firm sites lose ground without noticing. A website builder might create tag pages, author pages and date archives that all repeat the same short excerpts. Or a firm might publish twenty city pages that differ only by the city name. Google tends to pick one version and ignore the rest.

Google Search Console shows this stage directly. Its Page indexing report lists which URLs are indexed and gives a reason for each one that is not. Reasons such as "Crawled - currently not indexed" or "Duplicate without user-selected canonical" point you toward the fix.

Stage three: Google chooses what to show

When someone types "assault lawyer near me" or "what happens after a second DUI arrest," Google pulls matching pages from its index and puts them in order. Google says it weighs many factors and does not publish a complete list. Relevance to the meaning of the search matters. So does the searcher's location for local queries, which is why a firm can rank in one town and disappear in the next one over.

For local searches, the map results draw heavily on the Google Business Profile, including its category, its distance from the searcher and its reviews. The regular results below the map lean more on the website itself. Defense lawyers often treat these as one thing. They are related, but you can rank well in one and poorly in the other.

Matching a problem to its stage

Once you know the stages, most SEO complaints sort themselves out.

  1. "Our new page doesn't show up at all." Start with discovery and indexing. The URL Inspection tool in Search Console will say whether Google has crawled and indexed it.
  2. "Google shows the page, but on page four." That is a ranking question. Look at whether the page answers what searchers want and whether better pages already exist.
  3. "We rank downtown but not in the suburbs." That is usually about location and the map, and rewriting website copy will not change it much.
  4. "Traffic dropped overnight." Check whether pages fell out of the index before assuming a penalty.

This sorting habit will save you from paying for the wrong fix. A firm that rewrites copy for a page Google never indexed has spent money on a stage that was working fine.

What to ask your SEO provider

You do not need to learn the technical work yourself. You do need enough vocabulary to ask good questions and read the answers. Ask which stage each recommended task affects. Ask to see the Search Console indexing numbers before and after major changes. Ask what will be measured to judge whether a ranking change helped.

If any part of the work touches your advertising, such as new practice area claims or client reviews, check your state bar's advertising rules before it goes live. Those rules apply to web pages the same way they apply to print ads.

Questions about how Google ranks pages

How long does it take Google to index a new charge page?

It varies. On an established site, a page linked from the menu and listed in the sitemap is often picked up within days. A new site with few links can take weeks. Requesting indexing in Search Console can help with a single page, but it does not guarantee anything.

Does paying for Google Ads help organic rankings?

Google states that advertising does not affect organic rankings. Ads and the regular results run on separate systems.

Why does my firm rank differently on my phone than at the office?

Results change with location and device, and sometimes with small differences in wording. Checking from one spot gives you one view. Search Console averages across many real searches and gives a more reliable picture.
