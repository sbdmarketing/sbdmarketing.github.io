Helping Charge Pages Rank for Statute Number Searches

Some people search for a charge the way it appears on their paperwork. A citation or booking sheet lists a code section, so the first search is often that number plus a word or two: "PC 459," "VC 23152 first offense," "11350 HS lawyer." These searches are short and look odd next to the plain-English phrases most defense firms target. They come from people holding the document that started the case, which tells you a lot about where they are in the process.

A charge page can rank for both kinds of search if the statute number is treated as part of the page's subject instead of a footnote. Below is a practical way to research those numbers, decide where they go, and keep the page readable for someone who has never been inside a courtroom.

Find out which numbers people in your state type

Start with your own data. In Search Console, open the Performance report, filter queries by a few digits from a statute you handle, and look at what comes back. You will often see several spellings of the same section: with and without the code abbreviation, with "section" spelled out, with a space or a period in odd places. Each spelling is a clue about how searchers in your state refer to the law.

If your site is new and has little data, check how the courts and local news outlets in your state write the citation. Searchers tend to copy what is printed in front of them. Keep the list short. One charge usually has two or three common forms worth covering, plus a few odd ones you can ignore.

Put the number where readers and Google expect it

The statute number belongs in a handful of places:

  • The title tag, after the plain name of the charge, when the number is how people commonly refer to it in your state
  • The first paragraph, in a sentence that names the charge and the code section together
  • One subheading, usually the section that explains what the prosecution has to prove
  • The meta description, if there is room after the plain-language summary

That is enough. Repeating the number in every paragraph makes the page harder to read and does nothing for rankings. A reader who arrived by searching "PC 459" needs to see the number early to know they are in the right place. After that, they want to understand what it means.

Explain the statute in plain words right after naming it

The weakest statute pages paste the text of the law and stop. Statutory language is written for lawyers and judges. A person searching a code section at midnight is usually trying to answer two simpler questions: what does this charge mean, and how serious is it?

So name the section, then translate it. "Penal Code 459 is California's burglary statute. In plain terms, it covers entering a building or certain other places with the intent to commit theft or a felony inside." Then move on to what a reader cares about, such as degrees of the offense and possible penalties. Keep quotations from the statute short and clearly marked, and compare them against the official text before publishing. Legislatures renumber and amend sections, and an outdated quote on a charge page is worse than none.

Handle related sections without splitting the page too thin

Many charges come with neighbors. A DUI statute may have separate provisions for impaired driving and for driving over the legal blood alcohol limit, and a drug possession section often sits next to a possession-for-sale section. You have to decide whether each number gets its own page.

A workable rule: if the sections lead to different penalties, different defenses or a different kind of client, give them separate pages and link them to each other. If they are almost always charged together and the reader's questions are the same, cover them on one page and mention both numbers near the top. Before you create a new page, check whether an existing page already ranks for that number. Two pages chasing the same code section tend to split the clicks, and neither does well.

For the step before this one, there is a useful guide on picking the main search phrase for each charge page that pairs well with statute research.

Write for the reader holding the paperwork

Someone searching a statute number may be reading it off a citation or a letter from the prosecutor's office. Think about what they are likely to see next. A short section explaining, in general terms, what those documents usually mean and when a lawyer typically gets involved will keep that reader on the page.

Avoid promising outcomes. Statements about results get close attention from regulators, and lawyer advertising rules vary from state to state, so review your state bar's rules before you publish anything describing what the firm can do for a client.

Check the results after a few weeks

Once the page is updated, give it time, then return to Search Console. Filter the page's queries for the digits of the statute and compare clicks and impressions with the period before the change. If impressions rise but clicks do not, look at the title tag and meta description. If nothing moves, confirm that the page is indexed and that no other page on the site competes for the same number.

Questions defense lawyers ask about statute number searches

Should the URL include the statute number?

It can, but it does not have to. A readable URL such as /burglary-pc-459/ is fine. If an existing page already ranks under a different URL, changing it to add the number usually is not worth the redirect risk.

Do statute numbers matter outside California?

Yes. Most states have code sections that people learn from their paperwork, though the format differs. Some states use long numbers with dashes or periods, and searchers often drop those characters. Check your own query data instead of assuming one format.

Is it safe to quote the statute on the page?

Short quotations are generally fine and can help readers. Mark them clearly, check them against the current official text, and review the page whenever the legislature changes the law.
