SEO Terms a Defense Lawyer Should Know Before Hiring Help

Most defense lawyers meet SEO vocabulary for the first time in a sales call. Someone mentions crawl budget, domain authority and topical clusters within ten minutes, and the lawyer nods along while trying to decide whether any of it matters. That is a bad position to buy from. You end up judging the vendor on confidence instead of substance.

You do not need to learn the trade. You need about a dozen terms well enough to ask a follow-up question and recognize a vague answer. The list below is the set that comes up most often for criminal defense practices, with a note on why each one matters to a firm that wants calls about the charges it handles.

Words about how Google finds your pages

Crawling is Google's software visiting a page and reading it. Indexing is Google deciding to store that page so it can appear in results. A page can be crawled and still left out of the index, which surprises many lawyers who assume publishing a page means it is searchable.

A sitemap is a file that lists the pages you want found. It helps, but it is a suggestion. Google still decides what to index based on quality and duplication.

Robots.txt is a small file that tells crawlers which areas of a site to skip. A single wrong line there can hide an entire site, so it belongs on the list of things to check after any redesign.

A canonical tag tells Google which version of a page is the main one when several look alike. Defense sites that copied the same DUI page across twenty city pages often have canonical problems without knowing it.

Words about the pages themselves

A title tag is the clickable headline in search results. It is usually the most important line of text on a charge page from a search point of view, and it should name the charge and the place in plain words.

A meta description is the short summary under that headline. Google often rewrites it, yet a clear one still affects whether a worried person clicks.

Search intent is what the person typing a query wants. Someone searching "first offense DUI penalties" wants information. Someone searching "DUI lawyer near me" wants a lawyer. A good agency matches each page to one kind of intent instead of stuffing both into the same page.

Internal links are links from one page on your site to another. They tell Google which pages you consider important and help a reader move from a general page about theft charges to a specific page about shoplifting.

Words about local results

The map pack, sometimes called the local pack, is the group of businesses shown with a map near the top of many local searches. For defense firms it often brings more calls than the regular results below it.

Your Google Business Profile is the listing that feeds the map pack. Categories, hours, reviews and the address on that profile all affect when and where your firm appears.

NAP stands for name, address and phone number. Agencies care whether those details match across directories and your own site, because mismatches can confuse search engines about which firm is which.

Words about measurement

Impressions count how often your page appeared in results. Clicks count how often someone chose it. Average position is a rough figure for where you ranked, averaged over many searches, and it can mislead when a page ranks for hundreds of different queries.

Google Search Console is the free tool that reports those numbers straight from Google. If an agency does not ask for access to it, ask why. For a walk through the first report you are likely to receive, this checklist for reading your first SEO report as a defense lawyer covers what to look at and what to ignore.

A conversion is whatever action you count as success, usually a call or a form. Rankings and traffic matter only as far as they lead to conversions from the kinds of cases you want.

Terms that deserve a careful follow-up

Some phrases sound technical while saying very little. When you hear these, ask the speaker to explain what they will actually do:

  • Domain authority, which is a score invented by a software company and not a Google measurement
  • Guaranteed first page rankings, which no honest vendor can promise
  • Link building packages sold by the number of links
  • Content at scale, which often means many thin pages that read alike

None of these terms is automatically a warning sign. The problem is when the explanation stays abstract. A good answer will connect the idea to a specific page on your site and a specific charge you defend.

Questions about SEO terms

Do I need to understand technical SEO to hire well?

No. You need to know enough to ask what a change is for and how you will see the result. A vendor who can explain that clearly is more useful than one who uses more jargon.

Which number should I watch first?

Calls and forms from the pages you care about. Search Console clicks to your main charge pages are a good second measure because they come straight from Google.

Are ranking reports reliable?

They are snapshots. Results shift by device, location and time of day, so treat a single ranking report as a sample. Trends over several months tell you more.
