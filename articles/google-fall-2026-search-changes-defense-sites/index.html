What Google's Fall 2026 Search Changes Mean for Defense Sites

October was a busy month for anyone who watches Google closely. A spam update finished rolling out, several days of ranking swings went unexplained, and Google quietly revised parts of its documentation for site owners. Most defense lawyers never saw any of it, and that is fine. What matters is whether any of it touches the pages that bring in calls.

This note sorts the recent changes into two piles: the ones a criminal defense firm should act on and the ones it can safely ignore. The source for most items is Search Engine Roundtable's November 2026 monthly recap, which collects Google announcements and documentation edits in one place. Where a detail was unclear, it is left out rather than guessed.

The September spam update is finished

Google's September 2026 spam update completed its rollout in October. Spam updates target practices that break Google's spam policies, such as scaled pages made only to rank, hidden text, link schemes and expired domains bought to borrow their old reputation.

For most defense firms this update is background noise. If your site lost visibility in the second half of September and did not recover, it is worth asking a blunt question about past work. Were city pages produced in bulk with only the town name swapped? Did an earlier vendor buy links from directories nobody visits? Those are the patterns spam updates are built to catch. Recovery after a spam action is slow, and it starts with removing the problem rather than adding more pages.

Ranking swings without a named update

Tracking tools showed heavy movement around October 20, 21 and 24, and Google did not confirm an update on those dates. The same recap noted that the industry was still waiting for the next core update.

Unconfirmed swings tempt people to rewrite pages in a panic. A better habit is to wait two or three weeks, then compare clicks and impressions for your main charge pages in Search Console against the same period before the swing. If a DUI page dropped and stayed down while the rest of the site held steady, look at that page and the pages now outranking it. If everything moved together and drifted back, there is probably nothing to fix.

Documentation edits worth reading

Google's guidance on content made with generative AI now tells site owners to fact-check and review that content before publishing. The recap also reported a new warning about deceptive authorship, meaning bylines or author details that mislead readers about who wrote something.

Both edits matter for a defense firm. Many firms now draft charge pages with AI help, and a wrong statement about a sentencing range or a license suspension period does real harm to a reader who trusts it. A few habits keep you on the right side of this guidance:

  • Have a lawyer read every page that states a rule, penalty or deadline before it goes live.
  • Put a real attorney's name on legal content only if that attorney reviewed it.
  • Keep a simple log of who reviewed each page and when.
  • Revisit pages when your state changes a statute or a court rule.

None of this is new advice. Google has simply written it down more plainly.

Search Console gets a small but useful filter

Search Console added the ability to select several countries at once in its performance filters. For a firm that serves one city, this sounds irrelevant. It helps in one specific case: if your reports show a pile of impressions from outside the United States, you can now exclude them in a single step and see what local searchers are doing. Foreign impressions often come from scraped content or odd queries and can make a page look busier than it is.

If you have never opened Search Console, start with the performance report, filter to a single charge page and read the queries. That alone will tell you more than most monthly SEO reports.

Local profile changes to keep an eye on

The recap also listed several Google Business Profile changes, including tests of more detailed performance reports and new messaging features, and it noted that review appeals were delayed again. Defense firms should watch for two things. First, any new messaging option needs a plan for who answers it and how quickly, because an unanswered message from a person in custody's family is a lost case. Second, if a review you believe breaks Google's policies is stuck in an appeal, keep a record and keep replying to legitimate reviews in the meantime. Replies must never reveal confidential details, and your state bar's advertising rules still apply to anything you post.

What to do this month

Keep the list short. Check your main charge pages in Search Console for any drop that lasted more than two weeks. Read through AI-assisted pages for legal accuracy and add a reviewing attorney where one is missing. Decide who answers new message channels on your Business Profile.

Questions about recent Google changes

Did the September spam update hurt sites that use AI writing?

Spam updates target spammy practices, such as mass-produced pages with little value. A page drafted with AI help and carefully reviewed by a lawyer is a different thing from hundreds of near-identical pages made only to rank.

Should I rewrite pages after an unconfirmed ranking drop?

Usually not right away. Wait a few weeks, compare the same date ranges in Search Console and look at what now ranks above you before changing anything.

Do I need to list an author on every page?

Not every page, but legal content benefits from a real, named reviewer. Never name an attorney who did not review the page, since that is exactly the kind of misleading authorship Google warns about.
